# Voice

The Suite Book sounds like the hotels it lists: quiet, exact, assured. The reader is choosing where to spend US$2,000 to US$20,000 a night, or imagining it. Nothing shouts.

- Understatement. A fact carries more weight than an adjective: "Forty-six of the seventy-two rooms are suites" says more than "spacious and elegant".
- No superlative without the fact behind it. Not "the finest spa in Paris", but "a 1,000 square metre spa by Dior, with a 30-metre pool under the courtyard".
- Say what the place is, not how it is known. Never "the hotel says", "according to", "the website lists". State it.
- Never mention what is missing. If a fact is unknown, leave it out of the sentence.
- No methodology in the copy (checked, verified, sourced). Sources live in the Sources list on each hotel page and on the About page.
- No hype words: stunning, breathtaking, luxurious, nestled, boasts, iconic, world-class, unparalleled, curated, elevated, paradise, oasis, haven, exquisite, indulge. No em dashes, no first person, no exclamation marks.
- Particular over general: the name of the suite, the chef, the number of villas, the minutes from the airport, the view from the terrace.
- Tagline: one line under 90 characters. "Seventy-two rooms over the Seine, beside the Pont Neuf."

## Descriptions, the finished shape

300 to 500 words, four to six paragraphs, separated by blank lines:
1. Answer first: what the hotel is and where, in two sentences a reader could quote (this paragraph is the page's lead).
2. The setting: what you see on arrival, the building or the land, its history if it matters.
3. Rooms and suites: the count, the feel, then the one or two suites or villas that matter.
4. Food and drink: the restaurants worth naming and who runs them.
5. Days: spa, pools, the beach or the slopes or the game drives, what the hotel arranges.
6. Who it suits, in one or two sentences.

Every sentence rests on the file's facts. Short is better than invented.
